## 5. On 29 June 2026 the Supreme Court held in Chatrie v. United States that police conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they obtained a person's phone location data from Google; Justice Kagan wrote for the Court; warrant particularity and good faith went back to the lower courts.

Verdict: overstated

Primary: Supreme Court of the United States, Chatrie v. United States, No. 25-112, slip opinion, decided 29 June 2026. https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-112_0am4.pdf . Docket: https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-112.html (disposition entries read; the docket page states the judgment was "VACATED and case REMANDED" and that "Kagan, J., delivered the opinion of the Court").
Note: the bound-volume form https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/609us2r64_7mio.pdf is in the search results; WebFetch could not decode it. The slip PDF was read through the Read tool.

Read: 2026-10-30
Quote:
- Syllabus, Held (p. 1): "Police officers conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they acquired Chatrie's location data from Google because an individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in his cell-phone location information."
- Syllabus, p. 5: "Because this is 'a court of review, not of first view,' Cutter v. Wilkinson, 544 U. S. 709, 718, n. 7 (2005), the Court leaves it up to the Court of Appeals to decide whether, at each step of the search process, the warrant satisfied the Fourth Amendment's requirements of particularity and probable cause." Disposition line: "136 F. 4th 100, vacated and remanded."
- Opinion of the Court, p. 32: "It is therefore now up to the Court of Appeals to decide whether, at each step of the search process, the warrant satisfied the Fourth Amendment's requirements of particularity and probable cause."
- Opinion attribution (syllabus p. 5): "KAGAN, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, in which ROBERTS, C. J., and SOTOMAYOR, KAVANAUGH, and JACKSON, JJ. joined."
- Other opinions (syllabus p. 5): "GORSUCH, J., filed an opinion concurring in the judgment." "ALITO, J., filed a dissenting opinion, in which THOMAS, J., joined as to Part I, and in which BARRETT, J., joined as to Parts II-B, II-C-1, and II-C-2." "BARRETT, J., filed a dissenting opinion."
- Facts (syllabus p. 1): the warrant was "directed to Google" and sought data on phones "located within a 150-meter radius of the credit union"; the process produced "anonymized location data" then narrowed to identifying information.

True as: On 29 June 2026 the Supreme Court held, in Chatrie v. United States, that police officers conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they acquired Okello Chatrie's location data from Google, which they obtained through a geofence warrant. Justice Kagan wrote the opinion of the Court. The Court vacated the judgment and remanded to the Fourth Circuit, which must decide whether the warrant met the Fourth Amendment's particularity and probable-cause requirements at each step.

Notes:
- Holding, author, and date: hold as stated. The vote in judgment was 6-3 (Kagan's opinion joined by four; Gorsuch concurring in the judgment; Alito and Barrett dissenting, with Thomas), per the syllabus and docket. The edition does not state a vote.
- Good faith: the remand language read (syllabus p. 5 and opinion p. 32) names particularity and probable cause only. Good faith is not named in the remand passages read. Good faith was a district-court ground (the district court found the warrant "plainly violates" the Fourth Amendment but denied suppression on the good-faith exception, per syllabus p. 2). The Court granted review "solely on the question whether the police violated the Fourth Amendment in obtaining Chatrie's location data", so good faith was not a question the Court sent back. I did not read every page of the opinion (pages 6-30 were not read), so I cannot rule out a passing good-faith reference.
- Scope: the warrant was a geofence warrant; the data was Google Location History, and the time window was two hours in the request (syllabus pp. 1-2). The edition's "a person's phone location data from Google" is broader than the facts but matches the Held line.
- The edition cites Wikipedia [17]; the opinion itself is the primary and is now cited.
