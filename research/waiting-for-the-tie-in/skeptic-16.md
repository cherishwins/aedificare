## 16.1. SunZia "about twenty" years (cites 42)
Ruling: **amended**

What holds:
- Hitachi Energy (cite 42), 19 Jun 2026, gives no SunZia duration. Reopened: https://www.hitachienergy.com/ca/en/news-and-events/press-releases/2026/06/hitachi-energy-celebrates-the-inauguration-of-pattern-energy-s-sunzia-project-now-delivering-clean-reliable-power-to-millions-across-the-american-southwest . Its only time lines are "the third major HVDC Light project commissioned by Hitachi Energy in the United States within the last 6 months" and "in its first year of full operation". The attribution to Hitachi fails, as the researcher said.
- Renewable Energy World, Sean Wolfe, 2 Jan 2024, verbatim: "After more than 17 years of navigating permits and approvals, Pattern Energy Group has closed $11 billion in non-recourse financing and started full construction of SunZia Transmission and SunZia Wind." https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/news/largest-clean-energy-project-in-u-s-history-secures-11b-in-financing-starts-construction/
- Pattern Energy, 18 Jun 2026, verbatim: Heinrich, "SunZia becoming fully operational is a milestone more than 18 years in the making"; he goes on: "Through a whole series of obstacles spanning over a decade and a half". Pattern's own text: "The fully contracted project advanced through years of development, permitting, financing, ..." https://patternenergy.com/sunzia-comes-online/
- Neither the RE World article nor the Pattern pages use "two decades", "about twenty", "2006" or "2015". I checked the RE World page for each of those terms.

What is wrong in the lane:
- The True as says the 17 years is "Pattern Energy's own figure", and Notes say "Pattern's 2023 '17 years' is the company's framing". Both are wrong. The 17 years is in RE World's narrative voice. The only link in that sentence is on "Pattern Energy Group", and it goes to Pattern's 27 Dec 2023 release: https://patternenergy.com/pattern-energy-closes-11-billion-financing-of-largest-clean-energy-infrastructure-project-in-u-s-history/ . I opened that release and it has no 17-year figure and no use of "permit". Its only years are 2009 and 2017: "the journey we started with management in the formation of Pattern in 2009 and when we made our first joint investment into New Mexico in 2017." (Yakov Tsveig.) The lane's own Primary line says "narrative voice", so the lane contradicts itself.
- Heinrich's 18 years runs to full operation in June 2026 ("becoming fully operational is a milestone"). It covers the whole project, not permitting alone. The True as should not put it next to a permitting figure as if they measured the same thing.
- Better primary: the Pattern 27 Dec 2023 release, now opened. It does not carry the figure, so RE World stays the only source for 17 years.

The verdict "overstated" stands. "About twenty" rounds up from "more than 17" (RE World, to construction start) and "more than 18" (Heinrich, to operation). Neither is the edition's cited source.

The page should say instead (True as): "SunZia spent more than 17 years in permits and approvals before full construction began in late 2023, according to Renewable Energy World (2 Jan 2024); at completion in June 2026, Sen. Martin Heinrich called it 'more than 18 years in the making.' Hitachi Energy's release gives no duration."
Notes should drop "(Pattern Energy's own figure)" and "Pattern's 2023 '17 years' is the company's framing". In their place: "The 17-year figure is RE World's own narrative; the Pattern release it links (27 Dec 2023) gives no duration." The negative "I found no source for a 2006 start date" is still open. I could not search, and none of the four pages I opened gives a start year.

## 16.2. $3M to $8.5M a mile; 536 miles a year, 2020 to 2024 (cites 43)
Ruling: **amended** (the verdict and True as are upheld; one note is added)

What holds:
- The cited cleanenergygrid.org URL returned HTTP 403 to me as well. I read the same PDF on the Grid Strategies host, all 17 numbered pages: https://gridstrategiesllc.com/wp-content/uploads/ACEG_Grid-Strategies_Fewer-New-Miles-2025_Rev-1.pdf . Cover: Shreve, Zimmerman, Kershaw, Gramlich, Grid Strategies LLC, "With support from Americans for a Clean Energy Grid", July 2025.
- Addendum, p. 16, verbatim: "According to the data, the United States built an average of 536 new miles of high-voltage transmission from 2020-2024, down from an average of 942 new miles from 2015-2019, and even further down from an average of 1781 new miles from 2010-2014." The addendum chart is titled "Miles of new 345 kV+ transmission lines built over the last 15 years (updated using July 2025 data)", which confirms the 345 kV+ scope.
- Also verbatim: p. 2, "In 2024, only 322 miles of new high-voltage transmission lines were completed..."; p. 16, "...for a total of 888 miles of new 345 kV+ transmission constructed in 2024."
- No cost per mile appears anywhere in the report. Its only cost metric is per MW. P. 2: "765 kV lines can deliver power at up to 75% lower cost per MW than lower-voltage alternatives like 230 kV". P. 11 says the same.
- To test the negative further, I fetched two other primaries. Grid Strategies' "Large-Scale Transmission Deployment Saves Consumers Money" (the report's fn 27, https://gridstrategiesllc.com/wp-content/uploads/GS_Transmission-Deployment-Saves-Consumers-Money.pdf) returned HTTP 404. MISO's Transmission Cost Estimation Guide for MTEP24 (the report's fn 3, https://cdn.misoenergy.org/20240131%20PSC%20Item%2005%20Transmission%20Cost%20Estimation%20Guide%20for%20MTEP24%20-%20Redline631529.pdf) has exploratory $/mile tables. On pp. 48 to 51 that I read: Table 4.1-2, new double-circuit 345 kV, runs "$5.5M" to "$6.4M" a mile. Table 4.1-4, HVDC 640 kV, runs "$5.1M" to "$6.3M". The highest value in the last column of the table ending on p. 48 is "$6.1M" (its header row is on p. 47, which I did not read). No "$3M to $8.5M" range appears in those tables. So the range has no source I could find, and "not in the source" stands.

What the lane missed:
- The report's body gives a different five-year average that the addendum supersedes. P. 5, verbatim: "Over the past five years, natural gas pipelines have averaged 534 miles per year, outpacing the 345-mile annual average for high-voltage transmission by more than 50%." The lane flagged 322 versus 888 for 2024 but not 345 versus 536 for the five-year average. Anyone reading the body alone will find 345, not 536.
- Minor: the addendum runs pp. 16 to 17, not just p. 16.

Add to Notes: "The report body (p. 5) gives a pre-revision five-year average of 345 miles a year; the addendum's 536 (p. 16) replaces it. The edition's 536 is the addendum figure. The report's only cost metric is per MW, not per mile; the MISO MTEP24 cost-guide tables I read (pp. 48 to 51) do not give $3M to $8.5M either."

## 16.4. Two to five years to permit and build a lateral (EIA; no cite)
Ruling: **upheld**

- I reopened the page. "Natural Gas Pipeline Development and Expansion" says it is "based on data through 2007/2008 with selected updates": https://eia.gov/pub/oil_gas/natural_gas/analysis_publications/ngpipeline/impex.html . The lane's three quotes are on the page verbatim: "takes an average of about three years from the time it is first announced"; "A FERC review of an interstate pipeline project takes from 5-18 months, with an average time of 15 months."; "Pipeline construction is usually completed within 18 months and sometimes in as little as 6 months."
- "Lateral" appears only as an expansion option: "Installing a lateral or extension off the existing mainline". No time is given for it. Neither "two to five years" nor "2-5 years" appears.
- On the negative, I opened the same text at EIA's archive path (https://www.eia.gov/naturalgas/archive/analysis_publications/ngpipeline/develop.html). It is the same page, with no lateral duration and no 2-to-5 range. I could not search for a newer EIA source, and no other EIA page was found.
- The True as is accurate and the smallest change. The edition's own chain value for gas is "yrs:3", which matches EIA's three years. The "two to five years" text does not.

## 16.5. 29 yrs average discovery to production for a new US mine (cites 90)
Ruling: **amended** (the verdict "overstated" is upheld on stronger grounds; the True as and notes (4) and (5) change)

Better primary, now opened: S&P Global Market Intelligence, "Mine development times: The US in perspective", June 2024 (Bonakdarpour, Hoffman, Rajan): https://cdn.ihsmarkit.com/www/pdf/0724/SPGlobal_NMA_DevelopmentTimesUSinPerspective_June_2024.pdf . The release links it. I read all 28 pages.
- P. 6, verbatim: "The US has the second longest mine development times in the world, at almost 29 years on average from first discovery to first production. ... This US figure assumes that currently non-operating properties in the US – projects whose development usually began decades ago but have still not become productive – start producing by 2030."
- P. 19, verbatim: "our sample is expanded from 127 mines to 268, including 13 mines in the US" and "Where startup dates are not available, we optimistically assume a startup date of 2030". P. 20 table: US "Operating mines that came online 2002-2023" = 3; "Non-operating mines (still in development, pre-production)" = 10; total 13.
- P. 17, verbatim: on operating mines alone, "the US appears to enjoy shorter average lead times than Australia and Canada: 13 years compared with 15 years in Australia, 16 years in Canada". The three are "Eagle (which came online in 2014), Ruby Hill (2007), and Pogo (2006)".
- So the US 29 years is an average over 13 properties, and 10 of them have never produced. Their end dates are projected or assumed. The lane's note (2) said the average is "partly an assumption". In fact 10 of 13 data points are not measured outcomes. This is the main scope fact, and the True as should carry it.
- P. 2, verbatim: "The study was supported by the National Mining Association of the US (NMA). The scope of the study was agreed with the NMA but the Association did not provide data or substantive input to the report." The release omits the scope clause, and so does the lane's note (3). The cited NAM article says the report "was paid for in part by the NMA."
- The release subhead the lane quoted is verbatim: "Development of a new U.S. mine—from discovery to production—takes nearly 29 years, on average". https://press.spglobal.com/2024-07-18-United-States-Ranks-Next-to-Last-in-Development-Time-for-New-Mines-that-Produce-Critical-Minerals-for-Energy-Transition,-S-P-Global-Finds . The edition's wording tracks that subhead and the cited NAM line ("it takes an average of 29 years to bring a mine online in the United States"). The overstatement is S&P's own headline framing, which the edition repeats without the sample caveat.
- Note (4) is wrong in its inference. The NAM page is dated 23 Apr 2024, "updated July 30, 2024", and it links a Reuters story whose URL carries 2024-07-18 and the June 2024 PDF. It reports the July release, and the April date is not evidence of a pre-release report. https://northamericanmining.com/index.php/2024/04/23/u-s-mine-development-timeline-abysmal-report/
- The True as says "July 2024 study". The report is dated June 2024, and the release is 18 Jul 2024.
- For the chain's copper row, p. 6 gives the US copper figure directly: "Copper ... taking 24.1 years. The equivalent figures in the US are 24.2 and 31.8 years" (gold and copper respectively).

The page should say instead (True as): "In S&P Global's June 2024 study (released 18 Jul 2024, supported by the National Mining Association), US mines averaged almost 29 years from discovery to production. The average covers 13 US properties, 10 of which have not yet produced and are counted at a projected or assumed 2030 start-up. The three US mines that actually came online since 2002 averaged 13 years."
Note (4) should read: "Cite 90 (NAM, page dated 23 Apr 2024, updated 30 Jul 2024) reports the July 2024 release; it links the 18 Jul Reuters story and the June 2024 PDF." Note (5) stays flagged as secondary and unopened.

## 16.6. 7 to 10 years to permit a US mine (S&P Global; IEA; no cite)
Ruling: **amended** (the verdict "not in the source" is upheld; the True as is replaced)

- S&P Global: the full June 2024 report (link in 16.5, 28 pages read) has no 7-to-10-year figure and no total permitting duration. Its one permitting time is p. 11, verbatim: "required processes under the National Environmental Policy Act that produce an environmental impact statement can begin, which the government estimates takes approximately four years." It also says plan-of-operations completeness "also often takes years". The release has no permitting sentence.
- IEA: https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026 and its /executive-summary page both returned HTTP 403 to me. I did not work round the block, so the IEA attribution is still unchecked, as the lane said.
- The lane's True as asserts "Industry sources ... put environmental review and approval for major US mining projects at about 7 to 10 years" on the strength of a study the lane marks "(secondary; not opened)". That breaks the imprint's rule: a True as cannot rest on a source nobody opened. The lane's own Notes add that the figure "covers a defined group of BLM projects and is not a nationwide average", and the True as drops that limit.

The page should say instead (True as): "No source opened gives 7 to 10 years to permit a US mine. S&P Global's 2024 study measures discovery to production, not permitting, and cites a government estimate of about four years for a NEPA environmental impact statement; the IEA report could not be opened (HTTP 403)." Add to Notes: "The 7-to-10 figure appears only in unopened industry and congressional secondaries, reported as covering a defined group of BLM projects."
