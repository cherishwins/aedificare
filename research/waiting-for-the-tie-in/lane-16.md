# Lane 16: Transmission and permitting timelines
Edition: "Waiting for the Tie-In" (15 Oct 2026). Lane checked 2026-11-01.

## 16.1. [11 · What it would take (I-marked); 03 · The chain (High-voltage lines)] And transmission permitting measured in years, not decades, because SunZia took about twenty. (attributed to Hitachi Energy (chain source)) (cites 42)
Verdict: overstated
Primary: Pattern Energy (via Renewable Energy World, Sean Wolfe, narrative voice), "Largest clean energy project in U.S. history secures $11B in financing, starts full construction", 2 Jan 2024. https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/news/largest-clean-energy-project-in-u-s-history-secures-11b-in-financing-starts-construction/ ; Pattern Energy, "SunZia comes online, transforming power delivery in the United States", 18 Jun 2026. https://patternenergy.com/sunzia-comes-online/
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: "After more than 17 years of navigating permits and approvals," (Renewable Energy World, 2 Jan 2024, sentence links to Pattern's Dec 2023 announcement). Pattern, 18 Jun 2026: the project "advanced through years of development, permitting, financing, construction and community engagement." Sen. Martin Heinrich, as quoted on Pattern's 18 Jun 2026 page: "a milestone more than 18 years in the making".
True as: SunZia took more than 17 years of permits, approvals and development before full construction began (Pattern Energy's own figure); its owner's June 2026 release gives no single permitting duration and a senator cited more than 18 years.
Notes: The cited source (42, Hitachi Energy's SunZia inauguration release, 19 Jun 2026) does not say how long SunZia took. I read it in full; its only timeline lines are the "first year of full operation" CO2 projection and "third major HVDC Light project ... within the last 6 months". So the Hitachi attribution is not supported. "About twenty" is not in any source I opened; the sources say 17, "more than 17" or "more than 18". The claim's own logic is also in tension with its number: 17 to 18 years is what the edition calls "decades" in ordinary usage, so the "years, not decades" framing needs its own support (not checked here). Pattern's 2023 "17 years" is the company's framing, not an independent measure. Secondary reports gave "15 to 18 years" and one "18 years"; I did not open those. I found no source for a 2006 start date.

## 16.2. [03 · The chain (High-voltage lines); 08 · Side by side (Grid)] $3M to $8.5M a mile; 536 miles a year built, 2020 to 2024 (attributed to Grid Strategies) (cites 43)
Verdict: not in the source
Primary: Grid Strategies LLC and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid (authors Shreve, Zimmerman, Kershaw, Gramlich), "Fewer New Miles: Strategic industries held back by slow pace of transmission, Rev. 1", July 2025. https://gridstrategiesllc.com/wp-content/uploads/ACEG_Grid-Strategies_Fewer-New-Miles-2025_Rev-1.pdf (the cited cleanenergygrid.org URL returned HTTP 403; the same PDF was reached through the Grid Strategies host)
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Addendum, p. 16: "the United States built an average of 536 new miles of high-voltage transmission from 2020-2024, down from an average of 942 new miles from 2015-2019, and even further down from an average of 1781 new miles from 2010-2014." Body, p. 2: "In 2024, only 322 miles of new high-voltage transmission lines were completed, the third slowest year of new construction for 345 kV and above transmission over the past 15 years." Addendum, p. 16: "For 2024, FERC now reports 334 miles of new 345 kV transmission lines were constructed and 554 miles of new 500 kV lines, for a total of 888 miles of new 345 kV+ transmission constructed in 2024."
True as: From 2020 to 2024, the US built an average of 536 miles a year of new 345 kV-and-above transmission, per Grid Strategies and ACEG (July 2025, addendum using FERC data).
Notes: The $3M and $8.5M per-mile figures do not appear in the report. I read every page of the PDF (cover, contents, pp. 1 to 16 and the addendum); no per-mile cost appears anywhere. Search for "$8.5 million" per mile with Grid Strategies returned nothing usable. The 536 figure is "high-voltage" meaning 345 kV and above only, and it is an average over 2020 to 2024, not a per-year series. The body's 322-mile 2024 figure was superseded by the addendum (888 miles in 2024); the edition should use the addendum if it uses 2024 at all. Lane 16 did not check the chain's cost-per-mile sources beyond this report.

## 16.3. [03 · The chain (High-voltage lines); 08 · Side by side (Grid)] China: 340 GW of west-east UHV transfer. (attributed to NEA (chain source)) (cites 47)
Verdict: overstated
Primary: China Daily, "China sure-footed in carbon peak goals" (Jan 14, 2026), cited by the edition as 47: https://global.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202601/14/WS6966f6f1a310d6866eb339f0_2.html ; the 340 GW figure is in China Daily, "Published 14:53, January 23, 2026": https://www.chinadailyhk.com/hk/article/627649 . No NEA primary document opened.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: China Daily, 23 Jan 2026 (as read on fetch): "West-to-east transmission capacity in China was 340 GW in 2025" (attributed in the article to the NEA, National Energy Administration). Cited page (47), subtitle: "Nation set to expand its 'west-to-east' power transmission capacity to over 420 GW by 2030."
True as: China's west-to-east power transmission capacity was about 340 GW at the end of 2025, per the National Energy Administration as reported by China Daily.
Notes: (1) Scope: "west-to-east transmission capacity" is the whole west-to-east power transfer program, not UHV alone; the edition's "UHV transfer" label is narrower or different. (2) The cited link (47) does not contain 340 GW; it gives the 2030 target of over 420 GW and no baseline, and does not name the NEA. The 340 GW figure is in a different China Daily article. (3) A People's Daily-hosted Hunan government post (3 Nov 2025) said the program was "expected to reach more than 340 million kilowatts" by year end 2025, up from 270 million kilowatts at the end of the 13th Five-Year Plan; it attributes nothing to the NEA. (4) A Chinese-language search found no NEA statement with the 340 GW figure; it returned a 2022 NEA target of 360 GW for 2025 and a 2030 target of over 420 GW from a January 2026 NDRC/NEA guideline. Those figures disagree with 340; the edition should not present 340 as an NEA target. The NEA primary remains unopened.

## 16.4. [03 · The chain (Gas pipeline)] Two to five years to permit and build a lateral (attributed to chain source: EIA) (cites none in list)
Verdict: not in the source
Primary: US Energy Information Administration, natural gas pipeline development page (no title date; states "based on data through 2007/2008 with selected updates"). https://eia.gov/pub/oil_gas/natural_gas/analysis_publications/ngpipeline/impex.html
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: "An interstate natural gas pipeline construction or expansion project takes an average of about three years from the time it is first announced until the new pipe is placed in service." Also: "A FERC review of an interstate pipeline project takes from 5-18 months, with an average time of 15 months." Also: "Pipeline construction is usually completed within 18 months and sometimes in as little as 6 months."
True as: An EIA page based on 2007 to 2008 data says an interstate natural gas pipeline project averages about three years from first announcement to service; it gives no duration for laterals specifically.
Notes: "Two to five years" does not appear on the page. The page lists "Installing a lateral or extension off the existing mainline" as an expansion option but gives no time for it. The page's three years covers the whole interstate project including construction, not permitting alone. A search for an EIA lateral permitting figure (one query) found nothing from EIA. The page is dated by its data, so a newer EIA source would be needed for a current figure. Not checked: state agency approval times (EIA says no data is available).

## 16.5. [10 · The other queue (stat); 03 · The chain (Copper and cable)] 29 yrs Average discovery to production for a new US mine. S&P Global. (attributed to S&P Global) (cites 90)
Verdict: overstated
Primary: S&P Global, "United States Ranks Next to Last in Development Time for New Mines that Produce Critical Minerals for Energy Transition, S&P Global Finds" (press release, PRNewswire), 18 Jul 2024. https://press.spglobal.com/2024-07-18-United-States-Ranks-Next-to-Last-in-Development-Time-for-New-Mines-that-Produce-Critical-Minerals-for-Energy-Transition,-S-P-Global-Finds?asPDF=1 . The study, "Mine Development Times: The U.S. in Perspective" (June 2024), was not opened.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: "Development of a new U.S. mine—from discovery to production—takes nearly 29 years, on average" (subhead). "The new report examines 268 mines around the world to determine average development times from discovery to production. Most of these mines are operating. However, some are non-operating: they are still in development and not yet productive. For these non-operating mines, the report assumes a start-up date of 2030."
True as: In S&P Global's July 2024 study of critical-mineral mines, the average US mine took about 29 years from discovery to production, counting mines still in development at an assumed 2030 start-up.
Notes: (1) Scope: "new mines that produce critical minerals" (the release's title); the sample is 268 mines worldwide, mostly operating, and the study covers cobalt, copper, gold, lithium, nickel, palladium, platinum, vanadium and zinc. Copper is only part of it, and the sample is not all new US mines. (2) The average includes non-operating projects with an assumed 2030 start, so it is partly an assumption, not a measured outcome. (3) The 2024 release says the National Mining Association supported the study and did not supply data. (4) The edition's cited source (90, North American Mining, 23 Apr 2024, updated 30 Jul 2024) quotes the same 29-year figure and the same 268-project sample; I opened it. Its date predates the 18 Jul 2024 release, so the cite is probably a report on the study before release, not the release itself. (5) A 2026 S&P Global Market Intelligence study cited in a secondary piece gives about 16 years across 232 assets (secondary; not opened); the edition should say which study it uses.

## 16.6. [03 · The chain (Copper and cable)] 7 to 10 years to permit a US mine (attributed to chain source: S&P Global; IEA) (cites none in list)
Verdict: not in the source
Primary: none found. S&P Global 2024 release (see 16.5) measures discovery to production and does not give a permitting duration. IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 (https://iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026) was not opened; search results did not show its permitting table.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: none. S&P Global's 2024 release has no permitting-time sentence; its one time figure is "takes nearly 29 years, on average" for discovery to production.
True as: Industry sources, including a National Mining Association-linked study (secondary; not opened), put environmental review and approval for major US mining projects at about 7 to 10 years; S&P Global's 2024 figure is discovery to production, not permitting.
Notes: Searches (one each): "7 to 10 years" permit US mine IEA; IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 mine permitting lead time. Results gave the 7-to-10 figure only through industry and congressional material and a secondary Skillings piece, which says the figure covers a defined group of BLM projects and is not a nationwide average. A Breakthrough Institute critique and a secondary report of an IEA 2026 finding ("15 to 20 years" discovery to production) were also in results and were not opened. A secondary Skillings piece says S&P's 2026 study found about 16 years discovery to production. The IEA report itself was not fetched; it is the one primary left unchecked for this claim. If the edition keeps the IEA attribution, its page should be read directly.
