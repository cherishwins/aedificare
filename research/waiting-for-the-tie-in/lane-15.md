Scope: the edition's own figures, chain data and model script, run at the defaults. Inventory: research/waiting-for-the-tie-in/CLAIMS.md (lane 15). Tools: WebFetch, WebSearch, Read, Grep. No shell was available, so the model was run by hand: the script logic in source/waiting-for-the-tie-in.html (lines 381-410) and work/sim.mjs were checked to have identical defaults and identical logic, then stepped through year by year (2026 to 2032). ## 15.1. The requests add up to more than five Texases.
Verdict: holds
Primary: Utility Dive, "ERCOT tells Texas PUC large-load queue reaches about 474 GW", Aug 2026 (secondary report of the ERCOT filing; the filing itself was not opened). https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ercot-texas-puc-data-center-audit/828472/ . Peak: ERCOT, "2026 peak demand records (preliminary)", all-time-records page. https://www.ercot.com/static-assets/data/news/content/a-peak-demand/2026/all-time-records.htm
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Utility Dive: "ERCOT interconnection queue requests total about 474 GW"; "approximately 90% are data centers"; "the queue is more than five times ERCOT's record peak electricity demand." ERCOT records page: the highest 2026 value in its monthly listing is "91,134 MW on July 22", and the page states "The unofficial demands reported during, or just after, an operating day are preliminary."
True as: The large-load requests in ERCOT's queue, about 474 GW, are more than five times the grid's record peak demand of about 91 GW (July 2026, preliminary).
Notes: 474 / 91.1 = 5.2. The arithmetic holds on the page's own figures and on the Utility Dive reading. The comparison sets requests against peak load, so it is a ratio of a queue to a load, not of two like quantities; the page's I/F framing ("A request is not a project") already says so in section 01. The 91 GW peak is preliminary per ERCOT. The 474 GW is the requests figure as reported; many requests are duplicates, which the page acknowledges.

## 15.2. At the defaults, transformers bind first, then the tie-in, then firm generation from 2029, when spare capacity runs out and the new turbine slots have not yet opened. The queue never clears. Stranded compute peaks at about 34 gigawatts.
Verdict: overstated
Primary: The edition's own model script, source/waiting-for-the-tie-in.html (script lines 381-410, slider defaults at lines 356-379), checked against work/sim.mjs (identical p defaults and logic). Local file, not a public URL.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Page, section 05: "At the defaults, transformers bind first, then the tie-in, then firm generation from 2029, when spare capacity runs out and the new turbine slots have not yet opened. The queue never clears. Stranded compute peaks at about 34 gigawatts."
True as: At the defaults the model's binding limit runs transformers (2026 and 2027), tie-in (2028), firm generation (2029), tie-in again (2030), then firm generation (2031 and 2032). The backlog grows every year and never clears (34 GW in 2028, 108 GW by 2032). Stranded compute peaks at about 34 GW, in 2029 and 2030.
Notes: Hand run at defaults. Backlog by year: 2026 11.0, 2027 22.6, 2028 34.2, 2029 49.4, 2030 64.6, 2031 84.4, 2032 107.6 GW. Stranded (chips delivered minus powered, cumulative): 13, 23, 29, 34.0, 34.0, 33, 29 GW. The numbers match. The sequence does not: the tie-in binds again in 2030, so "then firm generation from 2029" is a simplification. The 2029 firm-generation bind is 15 GW (4 GW headroom plus 11 GW turbine share), which is below the 17 GW tie-in, so the cause is the turbine share, not a clean "spare capacity runs out"; headroom reaches zero only in 2031. Stranded compute depends entirely on the 32 GW a year chip-delivery input, which is labelled on the page as an assumption ("Assumption scaled to 2026 capex guidance of about $700B."). Nothing was run for other inputs.

## 15.3. The 2028 backlog in the model, about 34 gigawatts, sits close to Morgan Stanley's reported estimate of a 32 gigawatt shortfall through 2028, which the model was not tuned to.
Verdict: overstated
Primary: Morgan Stanley's own report was not opened (no primary reachable). Secondary: Benzinga, "Nvidia, Broadcom May Be Surprisingly Safe From a 32-GW Hole in the AI Boom, Morgan Stanley Says", Oct 2026. The fetch returned HTTP 403 and the text could not be read; the figure was taken from a search summary of that page and from other secondary coverage. https://www.benzinga.com/markets/prediction-markets/26/10/62174172/nvidia-broadcom-ai-power-shortage (page cited by the edition; the search result URL was https://www.benzinga.com/node/62174172). Model: local script, as in 15.2.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Page, section 05: "The 2028 backlog in the model, about 34 gigawatts, sits close to Morgan Stanley's reported estimate of a 32 gigawatt shortfall through 2028, which the model was not tuned to." Page, demand slider note (source/waiting-for-the-tie-in.html line 356): "Morgan Stanley’s reported 32 GW shortfall on 34% of demand implies about 94 GW sought over 2026 to 2028."
True as: The model's 2028 backlog, about 34 GW, is close to Morgan Stanley's reported 32 GW net shortfall through 2028. The model's demand input was read off that same estimate, so the match is not an independent test.
Notes: The closeness holds (34.2 against 32, a gap of about 2 GW). "Not tuned to" is not supported: the page's own demand-slider note derives its implied demand (about 94 GW over 2026 to 2028) from the Morgan Stanley figure, and the model's demand default (30 GW growing 12 a year) gives about 101 GW over the same years, so the input was informed by the comparison target. The Morgan Stanley figure is itself press-reported: the search summaries describe it as a net gap after on-site and grid alternatives, about 34% of projected demand, and say the original report was not checked; sources differ between 32 and 33 GW and give year-by-year figures that are ambiguous as to whether they are annual or cumulative. The model's 34.2 GW is a cumulative carried backlog, which may not be the same measure. Scope to state: a comparison of one model run against one press-reported estimate.

## 15.4. Defaults are set from the published figures above. Every slider says where its number comes from, and the ones that are my assumptions say so.

Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Each slider carries a note (the r[7] field), for example demand: "Morgan Stanley’s reported 32 GW shortfall on 34% of demand implies about 94 GW sought over 2026 to 2028."; growth: "NERC: 10-year summer peak up 224 GW, mostly data centres."; ERCOT: "Batch Zero: 66 GW conditional. Several defaults are my own choices that the notes do not derive from a published figure, and most of those are not marked as assumptions: demand (30 GW), growth (12% a year), ERCOT (6 GW), PJM (4 GW), rest of US (7 GW), spare firm headroom (10 GW), the 2028 transformer addition (8 GW), on-site ramp (3 GW a year) and the electrician pool (60,000). Notes: "Every slider says where its number comes from" holds (all 19 sliders have a note). "Defaults are set from the published figures" fails for the sliders above: none of their notes gives a calculation that yields the default (for example, NERC's 224 GW over ten years does not give 12% growth on 30 GW; Batch Zero's 66 GW conditional clearance is not a 6 GW a year rate; the electrician pool of 60,000 is not the BLS 821,000, and the note cites the national total). "The ones that are my assumptions say so" is partly true: the labelled ones are chips, turbine deliveries to 2029, share serving new load, transformers, electrician ratio (labelled as an assumption for the electrical share), residential share and homes per electrician. Of the unlabelled defaults, the notes give context, not provenance.

## 15.5. At that ratio, even the 66 gigawatts ERCOT just cleared would occupy a small share of American electricians at any one time.
Verdict: overstated
Primary: USC Hamm Institute, "data_center_workforce.pdf" (2025), the source for the one-peak-worker-per-MW ratio. Could not open: HTTP 403. https://hamminstitute.org/site-files/documents/data_center_workforce.pdf . Electrician count: BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook (source 22, not re-opened; the edition cites 821,000). ERCOT figure: Utility Dive, as in 15.1.
Read: 2026-11-01
Quote: Page, section 04: "A hyperscale site needs roughly one peak worker per megawatt." and "At that ratio, even the 66 gigawatts ERCOT just cleared would occupy a small share of American electricians at any one time."
True as: At one peak worker per megawatt, the 66 GW that ERCOT conditionally cleared would need about 66,000 electricians at peak, roughly 8 percent of the 821,000 electricians the page cites, if all of it were under construction at once.
Notes: Arithmetic: 66,000 / 821,000 = 8.0 percent. The ratio could not be verified because the USC PDF returned 403 and no other copy was opened. The page's own slider labels the ratio "USC: about one peak worker per MW at hyperscale; assumption for the electrical share," so the ratio is itself partly an assumption. "Small share" is the author's judgement; 8 percent at the moment of peak is not obviously small, and the sentence drops the "all at once" condition. The 66 GW is a conditional batch clearance, not a build schedule.
