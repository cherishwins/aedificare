## 10.1. Abilene: data-centre electricians about $35 an hour plus overtime and per diem; "a local homebuilder's electrical contractor" pays about $20 and lost five workers "to the site"
Researcher's verdict: overstated. **Skeptic: amended.** The verdict stands. The researcher missed a photo caption that shows the contractor works on new homes, so the rewording can be smaller. The researcher also missed the article's "double" sentence, which the chain line depends on.

Evidence (Sneha Dey, The Texas Tribune, "Data center boom strains Texas homebuilders' need for electricians", 28 Apr 2026, https://www.texastribune.org/2026/04/28/data-centers-texas-electricians-builders/):
- "The data centers outmatch him at $35 an hour, in addition to overtime and per diem benefits, he said." (speaker Scotty Wristen)
- "He can only afford to pay employees $20 an hour."
- Wristen, owner of WE Electric in Abilene, "lost five workers to the data centers" (plural).
- Photo caption: "Scotty Wristen, an Abilene electrician, wires a fuse box in a new south Abilene home on April 21, 2026."
- "The data center in the Abilene area, for example, offers electricians double the wages his subcontractors can" (about builder Gene Lantrip's subcontractors; sentence continues).
- "My electrician, he lost two of his lead men and several of his helpers to the data center," Lantrip said.
- Stargate is placed "On the outskirts of Abilene"; officials "aim to finish its construction by the end of the year".

Findings:
(a) The quotes in lane-10 are verbatim. $35 and $20 are Wristen's own figures, as the researcher says.
(b) The researcher is right that no sentence makes WE Electric "a homebuilder's" contractor. Lantrip's unnamed electrician is never identified as Wristen. But the caption shows Wristen wiring a new home, so "residential" or "that wires new homes" is supported. Saying "homebuilder's" is not. The researcher wrote that the only subcontractors in the piece are Lantrip's and did not mention the caption.
(c) "To the site" (one site) is wrong for Wristen: he lost workers "to the data centers". The singular "the data center" belongs to Lantrip's electrician.
(d) "Going up" agrees with the article's "aim to finish its construction by the end of the year". The article does not say Stargate is the *first* Stargate campus. I could not check that word: the search budget ran out, and openai.com returned 403. It stays unchecked, as the researcher left it.
(e) The chain line "data-centre pay about double residential in Abilene" rests on the Tribune's own "double the wages his subcontractors can" sentence, not just on $35 against $20. That sentence is about Lantrip's subcontractors, which is residential, so the chain line holds.

The page should say: "In Abilene, Texas, where the first Stargate campus is going up, data-centre electricians earn about 35 dollars an hour plus overtime and per diem, according to a local electrical contractor. He pays about 20, wires new homes, and has lost five workers to the data centres." The smallest fix: "A local homebuilder's electrical contractor" → "A local electrical contractor who wires new homes", and "to the site" → "to the data centres". On slide 08, "homebuilder electrician" → "residential electrician".

## 10.3. The country has 821,000 electricians, and about one in five is over 55
Researcher's verdict: overstated. **Skeptic: amended.** The researcher found that BLS has no age figure and that the "BLS" attribution is wrong for that half. That finding is overturned. BLS publishes electricians by age in its household survey, and its figure supports "nearly one in five". The same BLS table also counts a different total from the one the page uses, which the researcher did not test.

Evidence:
- BLS, Occupational Outlook Handbook, Electricians (last modified 27 Aug 2026): "Electricians held about 821,000 jobs in 2025." The page has no age statistic. https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm
- BLS, Current Population Survey, Table 11b, "Employed people by detailed occupation and age", 2025 annual averages. Row "Electricians": total 1,063 (thousands); 16 to 19, 21; 20 to 24, 146; 25 to 34, 261; 35 to 44, 243; 45 to 54, 200; 55 to 64, 156; 65 and over, 35; median age 39.6. Footnote: "Annual estimates for 2025 are 11-month averages that exclude October." https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11b.htm
- ABC via Construction Executive, 16 Jan 2026: "Approximately one-fifth of all electricians are over 55." No source is given for the figure. https://www.constructionexec.com/?p=62523

Findings:
(a) The age figure has a BLS primary. 156 + 35 = 191 thousand of 1,063 thousand, which is 18.0 percent aged 55 or older. "About one in five" rounds up; "nearly one in five" is exact. "Over 55" should be "55 or older", because the CPS band starts at 55. The researcher said the age figure is ABC's, not BLS's, but the edition's own lane brief names "CPS age data". The researcher wrote "I did not check the underlying BLS or ACS tables". This was the check that would have settled it.
(b) BLS publishes two counts. The OOH's 821,000 is jobs, from the employment projections. The CPS counts 1,063,000 people employed as electricians in 2025. "The country has 821,000 electricians" picks the lower count and presents jobs as people. The 18 percent share comes from the CPS base of 1.06 million, not from the 821,000. The two halves of the sentence use different BLS series. This does not change the share, but the head count should be stated as jobs.
(c) The year (2025) is missing from the claim, as the researcher says. The October 2025 gap in the CPS is a minor caveat.

The page should say: "Electricians held about 821,000 jobs in the US in 2025, and nearly one in five electricians is 55 or older." The same fix applies to the stat ("821k US electrician jobs. Nearly one in five 55 or older.") and the chain line. The slider's "BLS: 821,000 nationally" can stay, with "jobs" added. The source list should add BLS CPS Table 11b for the age half. ABC can stay as a second source.

## 10.6. Enforcement surges cut construction employment by about 2 percent in the cities they hit
Researcher's verdict: overstated. **Skeptic: amended.** The verdict stands. I checked the updated paper, which the researcher noted but did not open. It was published on 4 Sep 2026, six weeks before the edition, and it has no construction estimate at all. Its overall effect is also smaller than the original's. One of the researcher's quotes is also not verbatim.

Evidence:
- Original paper: Marcela Escobari, Ian Seyal and Paul Beach, Brookings, 29 May 2026. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/ice-enforcement-employment-effects-us-cities/
  - "Averaging out the effect in sector-level employment across all 86 surge cities" (continues "over the eight months following a surge in ICE enforcement, gives the estimates shown in Figure 5.")
  - "employment losses in construction averaged 2.2%, equivalent to 102,000 jobs across all 86 surge cities"
  - Overall effect: "observed between three and eight months after their local surge onset, employment averaged 0.73% below what would have been expected absent the enforcement shock." "The employment data also ends in September 2025."
  - Editor's note: "The authors have released an updated paper utilizing data over a longer time horizon and an improved empirical design." "The revised findings, methodology, and updated conclusions are available in" the updated paper.
- Updated paper: same authors, "Beyond arrests: How ICE enforcement depressed local employment in 2025", Brookings, 4 Sep 2026. Sample: 341 metro areas, 64 surge cities, data through December 2025. Overall shortfall: 0.43 percent six months after the surge. The page reports no sector or construction estimate; its only "construction" mentions are the title of a separate EPI study and, in the appendices, a cited estimate from other literature that 38 percent of job losses in construction fell on US-born workers. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/beyond-arrests-how-ice-enforcement-depressed-local-employment-in-2025/
- Appendices, Table 3. Preferred specification ("Intersection, X-13", 64 surge cities, 277 comparison cities): shortfall at 6 months −0.43 [−0.76, −0.10]. The original's 86-city definitions ("Absolute excess arrests" and "Per-worker excess arrests") are now listed as alternatives, giving −0.30 and −0.41. No sector rows. https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Beyond-Arrests-Appendices.pdf

Findings:
(a) The 2.2 percent is verbatim in the original. It is an estimated gap from a counterfactual, as the researcher says, and averaged over the eight months after the surge.
(b) The researcher wrote "I did not check whether the updated paper changes them". The updated paper does not re-estimate construction. It moves the preferred design from 86 to 64 surge cities and cuts the overall effect from 0.73 to 0.43 percent at six months. The edition is dated 15 Oct 2026, after the update, and cites a construction figure the authors have not carried forward. Its source page tells readers to see the revised findings. I make no judgement on whether a construction effect would survive the new design; the authors have not published one.
(c) Quote correction. The researcher's "Each surge city's post-surge window runs from three to eight months, with employment data through September 2025" is not on the page. The page has "observed between three and eight months after their local surge onset" and "The employment data also ends in September 2025."
(d) "Cities" are core-based statistical areas (metros). This is the authors' own usage, so it is not an error in the edition.

The page should say: "and a Brookings study in May estimated that enforcement surges left construction employment about 2 percent below its expected path in the 86 metros hit hardest, an estimate its authors' September update does not repeat." If the edition wants a figure from the current version: "and a Brookings study estimated that the hardest-hit metros had about 0.4 percent fewer jobs than expected six months after an enforcement surge." The researcher's rewording should also give the May date and note the update.
